Climate snapshot

Dallas summer risk — long hot season, humid spells, heavy PPE

Dallas–Fort Worth summers are long. Daytime highs commonly sit in the upper 90s and regularly pass 100°F in July and August, and warm nights limit overnight recovery during heat waves. Humid Gulf air on some days pushes the heat index well above the air temperature, while pavement, concrete vaults, bucket-truck work, and sun-baked substations add radiant load.

Utility work stacks extra heat on top of the weather: flame-resistant (FR) clothing, hard hats, gloves, rubber insulating gear, and respirators or confined-space equipment reduce how well sweat cools the body. Crews also work in spread-out locations — right-of-way, easements, manholes, rural lines — where shade, water, and EMS access are not guaranteed. Treat the worksite as hotter than the forecast, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT when PPE or humidity climbs.

Acclimatization

Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Most heat-illness events cluster in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into dispatch.

  • Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work loadCommon guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm the schedule against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
  • Experienced workers returning after leaveOften a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away, or moving from cool work to hot, as partly un-acclimatized.
  • Heat waves reset the clockWhen a sudden hot spell hits, treat everyone as partly un-acclimatized for the first days, including veterans.
  • Track itLog who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put that on the dispatch board, not in someone’s head.

Printable

Utility crew jobsite checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the truck binder.

  • Water at every siteCool potable water on every truck and staged at the work location, not only at the service center. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per worker per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes.
  • Shade / cool-downRight-of-way and substation work often has no shade. Bring a canopy, use a running truck with A/C, or stage a cooling area before high heat. Anyone can ask for a preventative cool-down rest.
  • PPE heat loadFR clothing, rubber gloves and sleeves, hard hats, and respirators cut cooling. Where safety rules allow, rotate tasks, vent or remove PPE during rest, and shorten work cycles when heat or humidity is high.
  • Buddy system and lone workersPair workers to watch for confusion, stumbling, heavy sweating or sudden stop of sweating, nausea, headache, or irritability. Do not leave a solo worker on a remote hot site without scheduled check-ins.
  • Remote-site EMS directionsBefore the shift, write down the cross streets, pole or structure numbers, GPS coordinates, gate codes, and nearest ER for each site. Know where cell coverage drops.
  • Confined spaces and vaultsHeat builds quickly in manholes and vaults. Limit entry time in heat, follow your entry permit rules, and keep an attendant who can call for help.
  • Training and languagePre-shift huddle: today’s triggers, water locations, stop-work authority, symptom reporting. Deliver in languages the crew understands.

Texas context

Local / regulatory note (high level)

Texas follows federal OSHA rather than a state-plan program, and the general duty to protect workers from recognized hazards, including heat, applies to employers of every size. Texas law (HB 2127, effective September 2023) also limits local governments from setting their own rest-break or water-break rules for private employers, so many Dallas contractors rely on their own written heat programs and customer or utility requirements instead.

Utility contractors often work under owner-utility safety manuals with their own heat language. Check what your contracts require, and keep a written plan and training log with the crew.

This is general awareness only, not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current federal and Texas requirements with counsel or your safety lead.

FAQ

Quick answers for Dallas utility crews

How fast should new utility workers ramp into heat?

A common approach is to start at about 20% of normal hot-work time and add no more than about 20% a day, with a faster ramp for experienced workers returning from leave. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

Does FR clothing change the break schedule?

Yes. Heavy or multi-layer PPE reduces cooling, so treat the heat index as effectively higher, shorten work cycles, and rest sooner. GOES digital WBGT helps because it reflects humidity and radiant load, not air temperature alone.

What are early signs of heat illness we train for?

Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, irritability, heavy sweating or a sudden stop in sweating, muscle cramps, and confusion. Cool the person, rest them in shade, give water if conscious, and call EMS for severe or worsening symptoms. Do not leave someone alone in a hot truck.

Does this page make us compliant with Texas or federal requirements?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your size, contracts, written program, training, and what you do on site. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
