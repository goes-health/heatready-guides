Climate snapshot

Phoenix summer risk — what crews actually feel

Phoenix sits in the Sonoran Desert. From June through September, daytime highs commonly reach the upper 90s to well above 100°F. National Weather Service climate normals for Phoenix (Sky Harbor area) put average July daily highs near 106°F, with June and August also frequently above 100°F. Overnight lows often stay in the 80s, so recovery between shifts can be limited.

The North American monsoon (roughly July–September) can raise humidity and dew points. That makes the same air temperature feel harder on the body — heat index and perceived strain climb even when the dry-bulb reading looks “familiar.” Pair temperature checks with humidity / heat-index awareness on monsoon afternoons.

Printable

Jobsite checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the gang box.

  • Water Cool potable water close to the work area. Aim for enough for about 1 quart (32 oz) per worker per hour, or a reliable top-up system. Encourage small sips every 15–20 minutes — not chugging only at lunch.
  • Shade / cool-down area Pop-up canopies, trailers, or building shade ready before high heat. Enough space for cool-down rests without crowding. Anyone can request a preventative cool-down rest; do not send symptomatic workers back until recovered.
  • Rest & work–rest cycles Schedule breaks that increase as heat index rises. Prefer earlier starts and heavier tasks in cooler hours. Reduce pace / rotate jobs on extreme days.
  • Acclimatization New hires, temps, and anyone returning from leave need a gradual ramp (often ~1–2 weeks of increasing heat exposure). Watch them more closely the first days on a hot Phoenix site.
  • Buddy system Pair workers to watch for confusion, stumbling, heavy sweating or sudden stop of sweating, nausea, headache, or irritability. Supervisors check in on high-heat days — especially lone tasks.
  • Emergency readiness Know who calls 911, cell coverage gaps, gate codes / cross streets for EMS, nearest ER, and cooling first aid (shade, loosen PPE if safe, cool with water). Never leave a symptomatic worker alone or send them home without evaluating care needs.
  • Training & language Pre-shift huddle: today’s triggers, water locations, stop-work authority, symptom reporting. Deliver in languages the crew understands.

Arizona context

Local / regulatory note (high level)

Arizona employers remain subject to federal OSHA (ADOSH) expectations under the General Duty Clause for recognized heat hazards, plus any contract-specific rules. The City of Phoenix has advanced contractor-focused heat safety expectations for covered City work (written heat plans, water, rest, shade / cooling, training, documentation). Statewide heat-safety guidelines have also been under active development — treat heat as a documented program, not informal advice.

If you bid or perform Phoenix municipal work, confirm whether the City worker heat-safety ordinance and flow-down clauses apply to you and your subcontractors. Keep the written plan on site and log what you actually did each hot day.

This is general awareness only — not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current City of Phoenix, ADOSH, and federal OSHA requirements for your jobs.

FAQ

Quick answers for Phoenix crews

How much water should we stage for an 8-hour shift?

A common planning figure is about 1 quart per worker per hour (roughly 2 gallons per person for 8 hours) if you are not plumbed — or start with less only if you have a reliable top-up route. Keep it cool and next to the work, not only at the trailer.

When should we treat the day as “high heat”?

Follow your written plan. Many outdoor programs escalate controls as heat index climbs through the 90s and into triple digits — and sooner with heavy PPE, roof work, or monsoon humidity. Check the worksite, not only the airport forecast.

What are early signs of heat illness we train for?

Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, irritability, heavy sweating or a sudden stop in sweating, muscle cramps, and confusion. Cool the person, rest in shade, hydrate if conscious, and call EMS for severe or worsening symptoms.

Does this page make us OSHA-compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Compliance depends on applicable rules, an effective written program, training, and what you actually do on site. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
