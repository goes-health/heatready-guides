## San Antonio summer risk: long hot days on exposed sites

San Antonio summers are long, hot, and often humid. Humidity slows sweat evaporation, so the body sheds
heat less effectively, and warm nights give crews less chance to recover between shifts.
Air temperature alone is a weak guide, which is why a humidity-aware metric such as WBGT is useful.

Oil and gas field work adds its own load: long shifts, little natural shade on pads and yards, hot steel
and equipment, flame-resistant clothing and other PPE, and tasks that cannot always pause.
Crews are often new to a site or returning from time off. Plan before the shift, and escalate using
GOES digital WBGT as sun and humidity rise.

- Humid: Slows sweat evaporation, so heat feels worse
- Long shifts: Hours of exposure on pads and in yards
- Shade point: Shade and water within reach of every crew

## Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Returning workers after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Contractors and rotating crews** — Crew arriving from cooler regions or a new contractor are the most at risk. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first task.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet or job safety plan, not in someone's head.

## Crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the site safety lead.

- **Cool drinking water, close by** — Plenty of cool potable water within easy reach of every worker. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Shade you can actually use** — A tent or vehicle large enough for the crew at once, placed near the work. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
- **Early start, heavy work first** — Where the job allows, move the most physical tasks to cooler hours.
- **Buddy checks** — Pair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. Workers who stop sweating or act confused need emergency help.
- **Clothing and sun** — Light, loose, breathable layers, wide-brim hats, and sun protection. Be aware that flame-resistant clothing and other PPE raise heat load.
- **Emergency plan** — Name the site address or GPS pin, the nearest road access for EMS, and who calls. Remote leases add minutes. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
- **Training in the crew's language** — Cover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

## Local / regulatory note (high level)

Employers have a general duty under federal OSHA law to protect workers from recognized hazards,
including heat. Check the current federal and Texas requirements, and any operator, contractor, or
site safety rules that apply to your crew, rather than relying on this page.

Oil and gas sites often involve several employers at once: operators, service companies, and
subcontracted crews. Clarify in writing who provides water, shade, and training, and keep a training log.

This is general awareness only, not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current
federal and Texas requirements with counsel or your safety lead.

## Quick answers for San Antonio oil and gas field crews

### Why use WBGT instead of air temperature for field work?

WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and air movement, not just air temperature. In hot, humid San Antonio summers, that gives a better picture of crew heat load, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a stronger trigger than the forecast high.

### When should we add breaks on a long shift?

Build water and shade breaks into the schedule before the heat peaks, and add more as conditions move into higher bands. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance for specifics.

### How much water should crew drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, more in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your employer, operator, site, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
