Climate snapshot

Houston summer risk — humidity + roof radiant heat

Houston has a humid subtropical climate. From June through September, daytime highs commonly sit in the mid-90s°F. National Weather Service climate normals for the Houston area put average July daily highs near 94°F, with June and August also frequently in the low-to-mid 90s. Overnight lows often stay in the mid-70s, so recovery between shifts can feel limited when humidity stays high.

Gulf moisture keeps dew points elevated much of the summer. That pushes heat index — what the body feels — routinely over 100°F even when the dry-bulb reading looks “only” mid-90s. On open roof decks, dark shingles and membrane add radiant heat that airport and phone forecasts miss. Pair heat-index awareness with worksite readings, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT when humidity and radiant load climb together.

Printable

Jobsite checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the gang box or staging trailer.

  • Water on roof / near ladder Cool potable water staged at staging and reachable from the roof (jug at the ridge or near the ladder — not only in the truck). Aim for enough for about 1 quart (32 oz) per worker per hour, or a reliable top-up system. Encourage small sips every 15–20 minutes — not chugging only at lunch.
  • Shade / cool-down area Open roof decks have almost no natural shade. Stage trailers, pop-up canopies, or vehicle shade at ground staging before high heat. Plan cool-down rests off the deck when heat climbs. Anyone can request a preventative cool-down rest; do not send symptomatic workers back until recovered.
  • Rest & work–rest cycles Schedule breaks that increase as heat index rises. Prefer earlier starts and heavier tear-off / install in cooler hours. Reduce pace / rotate jobs (ridge vs. eave, tear-off vs. cleanup) on extreme humidity days. Factor radiant heat from dark shingles and membrane — the deck runs hotter than the street.
  • Acclimatization New hires, temps, and anyone returning from leave need a gradual ramp (often ~1–2 weeks of increasing heat exposure). Watch them more closely the first days on a hot Houston roof — especially after rain when humidity spikes.
  • Buddy system Pair workers to watch for confusion, stumbling, heavy sweating or sudden stop of sweating, nausea, headache, or irritability. Supervisors check in on high-heat days — especially lone roofers, ridge work, and anyone on a separate elevation.
  • Emergency readiness Know who calls 911, cell coverage gaps, gate codes / cross streets for EMS, ladder and access path for responders, nearest ER, and cooling first aid (get off the hot deck into shade, loosen PPE / hard hat if safe, cool with water). Never leave a symptomatic worker alone on the roof or send them home without evaluating care needs.
  • Training, PPE & language Pre-shift huddle: today’s triggers, water locations, stop-work authority, symptom reporting, radiant heat from dark decks, and hard-hat / PPE heat load. Deliver in languages the crew understands.

Texas context

Local / regulatory note (high level)

Texas employers remain subject to federal OSHA expectations under the General Duty Clause for recognized heat hazards, plus any contract-specific rules. There is no separate statewide heat-illness standard analogous to Cal/OSHA 3395; treat heat as a documented program — water, rest, shade / cooling, training, and emergency response — not informal advice.

City of Houston and Harris County contractor / public-works expectations can include heat-safety language in covered contracts (written plans, water access, rest, training, documentation). If you bid or perform municipal or large commercial work in the Houston metro, confirm flow-down clauses for you and your subcontractors. Keep the written plan on site and log what you actually did each hot, humid day.

This is general awareness only — not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current City of Houston, Harris County, and federal OSHA requirements for your jobs.

FAQ

Quick answers for Houston roofing crews

Where should we stage water on a roofing job?

Keep cool water at ground staging and reachable from the roof — a jug near the ladder or at a safe ridge spot beats water that only lives in the truck. A common planning figure is about 1 quart per worker per hour. Encourage small sips every 15–20 minutes.

Why does the roof feel hotter than the forecast?

Dark shingles and membrane radiate heat; open decks have little shade; and Houston’s Gulf humidity pushes heat index well above the dry-bulb reading. Check conditions at the worksite — and escalate with GOES digital WBGT — not only the airport forecast.

What are early signs of heat illness we train for?

Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, irritability, heavy sweating or a sudden stop in sweating, muscle cramps, and confusion. Get the person off the hot deck into shade, rest, hydrate if conscious, and call EMS for severe or worsening symptoms.

Does this page make us OSHA-compliant in Texas?

No. It is an educational checklist. Texas employers follow federal OSHA (including the General Duty Clause for recognized heat hazards) plus any contract rules. Compliance depends on an effective written program, training, and what you actually do on site.
