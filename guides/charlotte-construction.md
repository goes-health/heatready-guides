## Charlotte summer risk: humid air and little shade on an open site

Charlotte summers are hot and humid, with warm nights and heat that tends to build over several days.
Humidity slows sweat evaporation, so the body sheds heat less effectively, and warm nights give crews less
chance to recover between shifts. Air temperature alone is a weak guide, which is why a humidity-aware
metric such as WBGT is useful.

Construction adds its own load: bare ground, slabs and steel radiate heat, open sites have little shade,
digging, lifting and carrying are heavy exertion, and PPE adds to the heat load. Conditions on site are
often hotter than the street-level forecast. Plan before the shift, move the heaviest work to cooler
hours, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT as sun and humidity rise.

- Humid: Slows sweat evaporation, so heat feels worse
- Open site: Radiant heat from slabs, steel and bare ground, little shade
- Laydown yard: Water and a cool-down spot staged near the work

## Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Returning workers after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Contractors and rotating crews** — Seasonal hires, crew arriving from cooler regions or a new subcontractor are the most at risk. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first task.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet or job safety plan, not in someone's head.

## Crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the site safety lead.

- **Cool water at the laydown yard and near each work area** — Plenty of cool potable water at the laydown yard and reachable from every work area, not only in the truck. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Shade you can actually use** — Open sites have little shade, so stage a canopy, trailer or vehicle shade near the work, large enough for the crew at once. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
- **Early start, heavy work first** — Where the job allows, schedule digging, lifting and material carrying for cooler hours and leave lighter tasks for the afternoon peak. Rotate people between tasks.
- **Buddy checks** — Pair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. A worker who stops sweating or acts confused needs emergency help.
- **Clothing, PPE and sun** — Light, loose, breathable layers, wide-brim or hard-hat brims, and sun protection. Hard hats, vests, harnesses and gloves raise heat load, so build in more cool-down time.
- **Emergency plan** — Write the job address on the crew sheet, name who calls EMS, and plan how EMS reaches an ill worker on a large site. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
- **Training in the crew's language** — Cover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

provides water, shade, and training, and keep a training log.

## Quick answers for Charlotte construction crews

### Why use WBGT instead of air temperature for construction work?

WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and air movement, not just air temperature. In hot, humid Charlotte summers, that gives a better picture of crew heat load, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a stronger trigger than the forecast high.

### When should we add breaks on a hot construction day?

Build water and shade breaks into the schedule before the heat peaks, and add more as conditions move into higher bands. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance for specifics.

### How much water should the crew drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, more in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your employer, client, property, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
