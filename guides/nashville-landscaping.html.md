## Nashville summer risk: muggy stretches, radiant heat off turf and pavement, little shade at the curb

Nashville summers are hot and humid, with many muggy days and warm nights that give crews less chance
to recover between shifts. Humidity varies day to day, and when it rises, sweat evaporates more slowly and
the body sheds heat less effectively. Air temperature alone is a weak guide, which is why a humidity-aware
metric such as WBGT is useful.

Landscaping adds its own load: hours of continuous exertion, mowers and trimmers that throw off heat, heat
reflected from driveways, rock and hardscape, shift routes that put crews in full sun at midday, and
little shade at a curbside stop. Conditions on turf and pavement are often hotter than the forecast. Plan
before the shift, move the heaviest work to cooler hours, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT as sun and
humidity rise.

Climate context based on general Middle Tennessee summer patterns. Always check the day's forecast and conditions at your location.

- Hot & humid: Muggy days slow sweat evaporation; warm nights limit recovery
- Exposed work: Sun on turf and hardscape; little shade at the curb
- Trailer & route: Water and a cool-down spot staged at every job

## Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Returning workers after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Seasonal hires and subcontractors** — Seasonal hires, crew members arriving from cooler regions and new subcontractors are the most at risk, especially at the start of the hot season. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first task.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet or job safety plan, not in someone's head.

## Crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the site safety lead.

- **Cool water at the trailer and at every stop** — Plenty of cool potable water on the truck or trailer and reachable from each work area, not only at the shop. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Shade you can actually use** — Lawns and curbs have little shade, so stage a canopy or the truck's shade near the work, large enough for the crew at once. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
- **Early start, heavy work first** — Where clients and noise rules allow, schedule mowing, digging, planting and hauling for cooler hours and leave lighter tasks for the afternoon peak. Rotate people between mowers, trimmers and hauling.
- **Buddy checks** — Pair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. A worker who stops sweating or acts confused needs emergency help.
- **Clothing, PPE and sun** — Wear the required protective gear, and use sun protection and light, breathable clothing where allowed. Hearing protection, chaps, boots and gloves add heat load, so build in more cool-down time.
- **Emergency plan** — Write the job address on the crew sheet, name who calls EMS, and plan how EMS reaches a worker on a large property or a back lot. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
- **Training in the crew's language** — Cover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

## Local / regulatory note (high level)

Employers have a general duty under federal OSHA law to protect workers from recognized hazards,
including heat. Check the current federal and Tennessee requirements, and any client, property manager,
or contract rules that apply to your crew, rather than relying on this page.

Landscaping crews often work for property managers, HOAs and commercial clients, and many crews use subcontractors. Clarify in writing who
provides water, shade, and training, and keep a training log.

## Quick answers for Nashville landscaping crews

### Why use WBGT instead of air temperature for landscaping work?

WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and air movement, not just air temperature. In hot, humid Nashville summers, that gives a better picture of crew heat load, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a stronger trigger than the forecast high.

### When should we add breaks on a hot landscaping work day?

Build water and shade breaks into the schedule before the heat peaks, and add more as conditions move into higher bands. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance for specifics.

### How much water should the crew drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, more in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your employer, client, property, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
