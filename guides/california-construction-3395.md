## California heat is not just the Central Valley

Construction crews work in very different heat across the state: Central Valley and Inland Empire
summers regularly pass 100°F, desert communities like Palm Springs run hotter still, and coastal sites
can swing from mild to dangerous when a heat wave pushes inland air to the coast. Steel decks,
concrete flatwork, asphalt paving, and roofing add radiant heat that a regional forecast does not show.

Cal/OSHA’s outdoor heat standard applies to outdoor places of employment, construction included, and
is built on four ideas: provide water, provide shade, take extra steps in high heat, and be ready for
emergencies, all documented in a written plan. This page turns those ideas into a one-page crew checklist.

Summary of the structure of Title 8 §3395 as described in public Cal/OSHA materials. Check the current text for exact thresholds and exceptions.

- 80°F: Shade must be available at or above this temperature
- 95°F: High-heat procedures apply at or above this temperature
- 1 qt/hr: Fresh, cool water per worker, per hour, over the shift

## Cal/OSHA 3395 jobsite checklist

Check these before the shift and again when it heats up. Print this section for the trailer or gang box.

- **Written Heat Illness Prevention Plan** — Plan is written, in English and the language most workers understand, and accessible at the site to employees and Cal/OSHA representatives on request. It names who is responsible and covers water, shade, high-heat procedures, acclimatization, emergency response, and training.
- **Drinking water** — Fresh, pure, suitably cool water free of charge and located as close as practicable to where people work. Plan for at least 1 quart per worker per hour for the whole shift, or a system to replenish it. Encourage frequent small drinks. Include multi-level and spread-out jobs, not just the trailer.
- **Shade** — When the temperature reaches 80°F, shade must be present and large enough for the workers on recovery or rest breaks, open to the air or cooled, and not blocked by tools or materials. Below 80°F shade or another cooling measure must be available on request. Workers may take a preventative cool-down rest of at least 5 minutes and should not be ordered back to work until symptoms are gone.
- **High-heat procedures at 95°F** — Ensure effective communication so workers can reach a supervisor; observe workers for alertness and heat illness signs, with a supervisor or a buddy system; remind everyone to drink water; and hold pre-shift meetings about high-heat precautions and cool-down rests. Review the current regulation for any additional requirements that apply to your operation.
- **Acclimatization** — New crew members and anyone returning after time away need closer observation, especially during the first days of working in heat. Pay extra attention during heat waves, when temperatures jump at least 10°F above recent averages. Pair new hires with an experienced buddy.
- **Emergency response** — Know who calls 911, how to give clear directions to the site (cross streets, gate entry, floor or laydown area), and how to reach a supervisor without cell coverage. Never leave a symptomatic worker alone. Provide first aid and cooling while waiting for EMS.
- **Training for workers and supervisors** — Before anyone works in heat: the risk factors, how water, shade, and rest work, how to report symptoms, how to contact EMS, and the plan’s acclimatization steps. Supervisors also train on the procedures they follow and what to do when a worker shows symptoms. Keep sign-in records.

## Local / regulatory note (high level)

California is a state-plan OSHA state, and Cal/OSHA enforces Title 8 §3395 for outdoor work. Indoor
workplaces are covered separately by §3396. Employers are expected to maintain a written plan and
apply it on every hot shift, including temporary and multi-employer worksites. General contractors and
subcontractors should agree on who supplies water and shade at shared sites.

The rules and thresholds can change, so confirm the current regulation text and any industry-specific
provisions before you rely on this page.

General awareness only: not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current Cal/OSHA requirements for your jobs.

## Quick answers for California construction crews

### At what temperature does Cal/OSHA require shade?

Shade must be present at 80°F and above, with enough capacity for workers on a recovery or rest break. Below 80°F, shade or another cooling measure must be available on request. Confirm the details in the current regulation.

### What changes at 95°F?

High-heat procedures apply: closer observation of workers, water reminders, effective communication with a supervisor, and pre-shift meetings on heat precautions. Check the regulation text for the full list that applies to your operation.

### Does this page make us compliant with Cal/OSHA?

No. It is an educational checklist. Compliance depends on your written plan, training records, and what you actually do on site. Use your safety lead or counsel for the formal plan.

### Our crew starts at 6 a.m. Do we still need all this?

Yes. Early starts help, but heat builds fast on slabs and roofs and many shifts run into the afternoon. Keep water, shade, and cool-down rests ready, and escalate when GOES digital WBGT or your worksite reading moves into the next band.
