Climate snapshot

Miami summer risk — humidity, warm nights, hot vehicles

Miami summers are hot and very humid for months, and nights stay warm, so the body gets little overnight recovery. Highs are often around 90°F, but with humidity the heat index regularly runs well above 100°F. Sweat evaporates poorly in humid air, which makes air temperature alone a weak guide.

Delivery work adds its own heat: repeated getting in and out of a parked vehicle, cargo areas and vans that heat up in the sun, dark asphalt and concrete at drop-off points, carrying loads up stairs, and uniforms or safety vests that trap heat. Drivers often work alone, skip water to save time, and have no shade on a route. Treat the route as hotter than the forecast and escalate using GOES digital WBGT when humidity and sun are both high.

Acclimatization

Acclimatization ramp for new and returning drivers

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on a route or after time away. Build the ramp into route assignment.

  • Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work loadCommon guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
  • Experienced drivers returning after leaveOften a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
  • Seasonal surge and new routesPeak-season temp drivers and anyone moved from an air-conditioned depot role to a route are the most at risk. Give them lighter, shorter-carry routes first.
  • Track itLog who is in a ramp and who their check-in contact is. Put it on the dispatch board, not in someone’s head.

Printable

Delivery driver checklist

Check these before the route and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the depot.

  • Water in the vehicle, every routeCool potable water within reach, with enough for the whole shift. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
  • Cab and cargo heatNever leave a person or pet in a parked vehicle. Park in shade when possible, use sun shades, vent or pre-cool the cab before getting in, and be careful touching hot steering wheels and cargo doors.
  • Cool-down between stopsUse a few minutes of A/C or shade after heavy carries or several stops in a row. Spread out the toughest stops across the shift.
  • Clothing and gearLight, breathable, light-colored clothing; hat or visor; sunscreen; vests only as needed. Check that required safety gear does not trap heat more than necessary.
  • Lone-worker check-insSet a scheduled check-in by phone or app. Dispatch should know a driver’s route and have a plan if a check-in is missed in high heat.
  • Know the symptomsHeadache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, irritability, heavy sweating or a sudden stop in sweating, cramps, and confusion. Stop, get cool, drink water, and call EMS for severe or worsening symptoms.
  • Training and languagePre-shift huddle: today’s heat triggers, water, stop-work authority, symptom reporting. Deliver it in languages the team understands, including English, Spanish, and Haitian Creole where relevant.

Florida context

Local / regulatory note (high level)

Florida follows federal OSHA rather than a state-plan program, and the general duty to protect workers from recognized hazards, including heat, applies to employers. Florida law also limits local governments from setting their own heat-exposure requirements for private employers, so many Miami delivery employers rely on their own written heat programs.

Many delivery drivers work as contractors or through platforms, which can change who is responsible for training and equipment. Check what applies to your relationship and keep a written plan and training log.

This is general awareness only, not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current federal and Florida requirements with counsel or your safety lead.

FAQ

Quick answers for Miami delivery drivers

How often should delivery drivers drink water in Miami heat?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, about a quart an hour in heavy heat, and not waiting for thirst. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

Why does humidity matter so much for Miami drivers?

Humid air slows sweat evaporation, so the body cools less. WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and wind, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a better trigger than air temperature alone.

Is a parked, running vehicle with A/C a valid cool-down?

Often yes, if the A/C works and the driver actually rests and drinks. Never leave anyone, including a person who feels unwell, alone in a hot vehicle.

Does this page make us compliant with Florida or federal requirements?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your size, worker classification, written program, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
