## Orlando summer risk: humid, long, and mostly outdoors

Orlando summers are hot and very humid, with a long warm season and afternoon storms that can leave the
air heavier afterward. Humidity slows sweat evaporation, so the body sheds heat less effectively, and
warm nights give crews less chance to recover between shifts. Air temperature alone is a weak guide,
which is why a humidity-aware metric such as WBGT is useful.

Landscaping adds its own load: hours of continuous exertion in direct sun, hot turf, pavement and mulch
beds, loud equipment with hearing protection, long sleeves or chemical-handling gear on some tasks, and a
tight route schedule that tempts crews to skip breaks. Seasonal and newly hired crew are often the least
acclimatized. Plan before the shift, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT as sun and humidity rise.

## Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Returning workers after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Contractors and rotating crews** — Seasonal hires, crew arriving from cooler regions or a new subcontractor are the most at risk. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first task.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet or job safety plan, not in someone's head.

## Crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the site safety lead.

- **Cool drinking water, close by** — Plenty of cool potable water within easy reach of every worker. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Shade you can actually use** — A tent, tree line or truck cab large enough for the crew at once, placed near the work. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
- **Early start, heavy work first** — Where the job allows, move edging, hauling and install work to cooler hours and leave lighter tasks for the afternoon peak.
- **Buddy checks** — Pair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. Workers who stop sweating or act confused need emergency help.
- **Clothing and sun** — Light, loose, breathable layers, wide-brim hats, and sun protection. Be aware that long sleeves, chemical-handling gear and hearing protection raise heat load.
- **Emergency plan** — Write the property address on the crew sheet, name the nearest road access for EMS, and who calls. Large or gated properties add minutes. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
- **Training in the crew's language** — Cover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

## Local / regulatory note (high level)

Employers have a general duty under federal OSHA law to protect workers from recognized hazards,
including heat. Check the current federal and Florida requirements, and any client, property manager,
or contract rules that apply to your crew, rather than relying on this page.

Landscaping crews often work under several clients and subcontractors in one week. Clarify in writing who
provides water, shade, and training, and keep a training log.

## Quick answers for Orlando landscaping crews

### Why use WBGT instead of air temperature for landscaping work?

WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and air movement, not just air temperature. In hot, humid Orlando summers, that gives a better picture of crew heat load, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a stronger trigger than the forecast high.

### When should we add breaks on a long route day?

Build water and shade breaks into the schedule before the heat peaks, and add more as conditions move into higher bands. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance for specifics.

### How much water should crew drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, more in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your employer, client, property, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
