## Tucson summer risk: dry heat, then monsoon humidity

Tucson summers are long and hot, with afternoon highs commonly above 100°F. Early summer is very
dry. Monsoon season, roughly July through September, brings higher humidity, which slows sweat
evaporation and can make a lower air temperature feel and act much hotter on the body.

Field work adds its own load: little shade between rows, hot soil radiating upward, repetitive
bending and lifting, long distances from water, and long sleeves or hats that help with sun but
trap heat. Crews are often seasonal and new to the climate. Treat any day forecast at 95°F or
above as a high-heat day, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT when sun is strong or humidity rises.

## Acclimatization ramp for new and seasonal workers

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Returning workers after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Seasonal and labor-contractor crews** — Workers arriving from cooler regions are the most at risk. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first row.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet, not in someone's head.

## Field crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the foreman.

- **Cool drinking water, close by** — Plenty of cool potable water within easy reach of every worker. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Shade you can actually use** — A shade structure or vehicle large enough for the whole crew at once, placed near the work. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
- **Early start, heavy work first** — Move the hardest tasks to the cooler morning hours and taper off before the afternoon peak where the schedule allows.
- **Buddy checks** — Pair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. Workers who stop sweating or act confused need emergency help.
- **Clothing and sun** — Light, loose, breathable layers, wide-brim hats, and sun protection. Be aware that protective clothing and PPE raise heat load.
- **Emergency plan** — Name the field address or GPS pin, the nearest road access for EMS, and who calls. Remote fields add minutes. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
- **Training in the crew's language** — Cover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

## Local / regulatory note (high level)

Employers have a general duty under federal OSHA law to protect workers from recognized hazards,
including heat. Heat-specific rules for agriculture vary by state and have been changing, so
check the current Arizona and federal requirements that apply to your operation rather than
relying on this page.

Many Tucson field workers are hired through labor contractors, which can change who is
responsible for training and water and shade. Clarify it in writing and keep a training log.

This is general awareness only, not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current
federal and Arizona requirements with counsel or your safety lead.

## Quick answers for Tucson agriculture crews

### What is a high-heat procedure at 95°F?

It is a common practice, used in several state programs, of adding extra controls once the temperature reaches 95°F: shade close by, more frequent breaks, buddy checks, and closer observation. Confirm what applies to you.

### Does Tucson's dry heat make it safer?

No. Dry air helps sweat evaporate, but workers can lose fluids quickly and not notice. During monsoon season humidity removes that advantage. WBGT accounts for humidity and sun, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a better trigger than air temperature alone.

### How much water should field workers drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, about a quart an hour in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your operation, worker classification, written program, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
