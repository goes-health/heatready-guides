## Miami summer risk — humidity, warm nights, hot vehicles

Miami summers are hot and very humid for months, and nights stay warm, so the body gets little
overnight recovery. Highs are often around 90°F, but with humidity the heat index regularly runs
well above 100°F. Sweat evaporates poorly in humid air, which makes air temperature alone a weak
guide.

Delivery work adds its own heat: repeated getting in and out of a parked vehicle, cargo areas and
vans that heat up in the sun, dark asphalt and concrete at drop-off points, carrying loads up
stairs, and uniforms or safety vests that trap heat. Drivers often work alone, skip water to save
time, and have no shade on a route. Treat the route as hotter than the forecast and escalate using
GOES digital WBGT when humidity and sun are both high.

Climate context based on general NWS / NOAA Miami climate patterns. Always check the day’s forecast and conditions on the route.

- ~90°F: Typical summer afternoon highs
- 100°+: Heat index on many humid afternoons
- Stop-and-go: Repeated cab, cargo, and sidewalk heat exposure

## Heat index break cadence for drivers

Example water–rest–shade cadence for delivery routes, aligned with common OSHA / NIOSH heat-awareness
practice. Add a band when drivers work in vehicles without working A/C, carry heavy loads, or run
back-to-back stops in direct sun. Where your program uses WBGT, escalate when GOES digital WBGT /

| Heat index (approx.) | Risk band | Suggested cadence & controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 80–90°F | Caution | Cold water in the cab; sip every 15–20 min; run A/C during breaks; watch new drivers and anyone returning from time off. |
| 91–103°F | High | Planned water and cool-down breaks about every hour in shade or an air-conditioned cab; pre-shift heat huddle; check-in calls for lone drivers; load heavy stops earlier in the day. |
| 103–115°F | Very high | More frequent, longer cool-down breaks; rebalance routes to cut back-to-back sun exposure and long carries; dispatcher check-ins; consider paired or shortened routes. |
| Above ~115°F | Extreme | Maximum controls; delay or reschedule non-urgent deliveries where feasible; keep cooling first aid and EMS readiness in mind for every route. |

## Acclimatization ramp for new and returning drivers

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on a route or after time away. Build the ramp into route assignment.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Experienced drivers returning after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Seasonal surge and new routes** — Peak-season temp drivers and anyone moved from an air-conditioned depot role to a route are the most at risk. Give them lighter, shorter-carry routes first.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their check-in contact is. Put it on the dispatch board, not in someone’s head.

## Delivery driver checklist

Check these before the route and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the depot.

- **Water in the vehicle, every route** — Cool potable water within reach, with enough for the whole shift. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Cab and cargo heat** — Never leave a person or pet in a parked vehicle. Park in shade when possible, use sun shades, vent or pre-cool the cab before getting in, and be careful touching hot steering wheels and cargo doors.
- **Cool-down between stops** — Use a few minutes of A/C or shade after heavy carries or several stops in a row. Spread out the toughest stops across the shift.
- **Clothing and gear** — Light, breathable, light-colored clothing; hat or visor; sunscreen; vests only as needed. Check that required safety gear does not trap heat more than necessary.
- **Lone-worker check-ins** — Set a scheduled check-in by phone or app. Dispatch should know a driver’s route and have a plan if a check-in is missed in high heat.
- **Know the symptoms** — Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, irritability, heavy sweating or a sudden stop in sweating, cramps, and confusion. Stop, get cool, drink water, and call EMS for severe or worsening symptoms.
- **Training and language** — Pre-shift huddle: today’s heat triggers, water, stop-work authority, symptom reporting. Deliver it in languages the team understands, including English, Spanish, and Haitian Creole where relevant.

## Local / regulatory note (high level)

Florida follows federal OSHA rather than a state-plan program, and the general duty to protect
workers from recognized hazards, including heat, applies to employers. Florida law also limits
local governments from setting their own heat-exposure requirements for private employers, so
many Miami delivery employers rely on their own written heat programs.

Many delivery drivers work as contractors or through platforms, which can change who is
responsible for training and equipment. Check what applies to your relationship and keep a written
plan and training log.

This is general awareness only, not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current
federal and Florida requirements with counsel or your safety lead.

## Quick answers for Miami delivery drivers

### How often should delivery drivers drink water in Miami heat?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, about a quart an hour in heavy heat, and not waiting for thirst. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Why does humidity matter so much for Miami drivers?

Humid air slows sweat evaporation, so the body cools less. WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and wind, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a better trigger than air temperature alone.

### Is a parked, running vehicle with A/C a valid cool-down?

Often yes, if the A/C works and the driver actually rests and drinks. Never leave anyone, including a person who feels unwell, alone in a hot vehicle.

### Does this page make us compliant with Florida or federal requirements?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your size, worker classification, written program, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
