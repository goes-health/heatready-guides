Climate snapshot

Las Vegas summer risk — dry heat + radiant ground

Las Vegas sits in the Mojave Desert. From June through September, daytime highs commonly reach the upper 90s to well above 100°F. National Weather Service climate normals for the Las Vegas area put average July daily highs near 104°F, with June often near 99°F and August also frequently above 100°F. Overnight lows often stay around 80°F in July, so recovery between shifts can be limited — especially with the valley’s urban heat island.

Desert air is usually very dry, so sweat evaporates quickly. Crews can underestimate how much fluid they are losing because they do not feel “soaked.” Rock, decomposed granite, gravel, pavers, stucco, and asphalt all add radiant and conductive heat that the airport forecast misses. The North American monsoon (roughly July–September) can raise humidity and dew points on some afternoons, making the same air temperature harder on the body. Pair temperature checks with worksite readings, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT when radiant load or humidity climbs.

Printable

Jobsite checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the truck binder or gang box.

  • Water at every stop Cool potable water in each truck and staged at the property — not only back at the yard. Landscaping crews bounce between HOA lots, new builds, and commercial sites. Aim for enough for about 1 quart (32 oz) per worker per hour, or a reliable top-up system. Encourage small sips every 15–20 minutes — not chugging only at lunch. Dry heat hides how much you are sweating.
  • Shade / cool-down area New desert developments and rock-and-gravel lots often have little mature tree shade. Stage pop-up canopies, trailer shade, or a cooled vehicle before high heat, and re-set shade when the crew moves to the next stop. Anyone can request a preventative cool-down rest; do not send symptomatic workers back until recovered.
  • Rest & work–rest cycles Schedule breaks that increase as heat index or WBGT rises. Prefer earlier starts (common 4–6 a.m. in valley landscaping) and heavier mowing, planting, or hardscape in cooler hours. Reduce pace / rotate jobs (sun vs. building shade, mowing vs. irrigation checks) on extreme days. Factor radiant heat from rock, gravel, and asphalt — the ground runs hotter than the street forecast.
  • Acclimatization New hires, temps, and anyone returning from leave — or new to Mojave dry heat — need a gradual ramp (often ~1–2 weeks of increasing heat exposure). Watch them more closely the first days on a hot Las Vegas route.
  • Buddy system Pair workers to watch for confusion, stumbling, heavy sweating or sudden stop of sweating, nausea, headache, or irritability. Supervisors check in on high-heat days — especially when the crew splits across properties or someone works a remote HOA lot alone.
  • Emergency readiness Know who calls 911, cell coverage gaps, gate codes / cross streets for EMS (new subdivisions can be hard to describe), nearest ER, and cooling first aid (shade, loosen PPE if safe, cool with water). Never leave a symptomatic worker alone in a hot truck or send them home without evaluating care needs.
  • Training, PPE & language Pre-shift huddle: today’s triggers, water locations, stop-work authority, symptom reporting, dry-heat dehydration, and extra heat load from long sleeves, chemical / pesticide PPE, or backpack sprayers. Deliver in languages the crew understands.

Nevada context

Local / regulatory note (high level)

Nevada is a state-plan OSHA state. Nevada OSHA’s heat-illness regulation (R131-24AP) has been in enforcement since April 29, 2025. Covered employers — generally those with more than 10 employees who must keep a written safety program — are expected to complete a job hazard analysis for classifications with substantial heat exposure and, if hazards are identified, maintain a written plan covering potable water, rest when someone shows symptoms of heat illness, means of cooling, monitoring of hot conditions, training, and emergency procedures. Employers of every size still have a duty to protect workers from recognized heat hazards.

Landscaping routes in the Las Vegas / Clark County metro often split crews across HOA, municipal, and commercial sites. Confirm any contract or flow-down heat-safety language for you and your subcontractors. Keep the written plan with the crew (truck binder or phone) and log what you actually did each hot day.

This is general awareness only — not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current Nevada OSHA, Clark County, and City of Las Vegas requirements for your jobs.

FAQ

Quick answers for Las Vegas landscaping crews

Why does dry heat still dehydrate us so fast?

In the Mojave, sweat evaporates quickly, so you may not feel soaked even while you lose a lot of fluid. Plan about 1 quart per worker per hour, keep water in every truck and at the property — not only at the yard — and encourage small sips every 15–20 minutes.

We already start at 5 a.m. Do we still need afternoon controls?

Early starts help, but many routes still run into peak heat. Keep shade, water, and longer rest cycles ready after mid-morning, and escalate when GOES digital WBGT or your worksite reading moves into the next band — not only because the clock says noon.

What are early signs of heat illness we train for?

Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, irritability, heavy sweating or a sudden stop in sweating, muscle cramps, and confusion. Cool the person, rest in shade, hydrate if conscious, and call EMS for severe or worsening symptoms. Do not leave someone alone in a hot truck.

Does this page make us compliant with Nevada OSHA’s heat rule?

No. It is an educational checklist. Nevada OSHA’s heat-illness regulation (R131-24AP) and the general duty to protect workers from recognized hazards depend on your size, job classifications, written program, training, and what you actually do on site. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
