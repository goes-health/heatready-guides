## Atlanta summer risk: humid heat that lingers into the evening

Atlanta summers are hot and humid. High humidity slows sweat evaporation, so the body sheds heat
less effectively, and warm, muggy evenings give crews less chance to recover between long days.
That makes air temperature alone a weak guide, and it is why a humidity-aware metric such as WBGT is useful.

Film and event work adds its own load: long call times, standing for hours, black tents and equipment
that soak up sun, heavy gear and costumes, asphalt lots and rooftops, and schedules driven by the shot
or the show rather than the weather. Crew are often on location for the first time in the season.
Plan for it before call time, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT as sun and humidity rise.

- Humid: Slows sweat evaporation, so heat feels worse
- Long calls: Hours of exposure on set or stage

## Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Returning workers after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Freelance and touring crews** — Crew arriving from cooler regions are the most at risk. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first setup.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet or call sheet, not in someone's head.

## Crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the production safety lead.

- **Cool drinking water, close by** — Plenty of cool potable water within easy reach of every worker. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Shade you can actually use** — A tent or vehicle large enough for the crew at once, placed near set or stage. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
- **Early start, heavy work first** — Where the schedule allows, move load-in, strike, and other heavy tasks to cooler hours.
- **Buddy checks** — Pair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. Workers who stop sweating or act confused need emergency help.
- **Clothing and sun** — Light, loose, breathable layers, wide-brim hats, and sun protection. Be aware that costumes, protective clothing, and PPE raise heat load.
- **Emergency plan** — Name the venue address or GPS pin, the nearest road access for EMS, and who calls. Remote locations add minutes. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
- **Training in the crew's language** — Cover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

## Local / regulatory note (high level)

Employers have a general duty under federal OSHA law to protect workers from recognized hazards,
including heat. Check the current federal and Georgia requirements, and any venue, union, or
production safety rules that apply to your crew, rather than relying on this page.

Productions and events often involve several employers on one site: producers, vendors, venue staff,
and subcontracted crews. Clarify in writing who provides water, shade, and training, and keep a training log.

This is general awareness only, not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current
federal and Georgia requirements with counsel or your safety lead.

## Quick answers for Atlanta film and event crews

### Why use WBGT instead of air temperature for a shoot or event?

WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and air movement, not just air temperature. In humid Atlanta summers, that gives a better picture of crew heat load, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a stronger trigger than the forecast high.

### When should we add breaks on a long call?

Build water and shade breaks into the schedule before the heat peaks, and add more as conditions move into higher bands. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance for specifics.

### How much water should crew drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, more in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your employer, production, venue, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
