Climate snapshot

Memphis summer risk: muggy stretches, radiant heat off slabs and steel, little shade on open sites

Memphis summers are hot and humid, with many muggy days and warm nights that give crews less chance to recover between shifts. Humidity varies day to day, and when it rises, sweat evaporates more slowly and the body sheds heat less effectively. Air temperature alone is a weak guide, which is why a humidity-aware metric such as WBGT is useful.

Construction adds its own load: hours of heavy exertion, heat reflected from fresh concrete, decking and roofing, protective gear that traps heat, and open sites with little shade at midday. Conditions on a slab or deck are often hotter than the forecast. Plan before the shift, move the heaviest work to cooler hours, and escalate using GOES digital WBGT as sun and humidity rise.

Acclimatization

Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

  • Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work loadCommon guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
  • Returning workers after leaveOften a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
  • Seasonal hires and subcontractorsSeasonal hires, crew members arriving from cooler regions and new subcontractors are the most at risk, especially at the start of the hot season. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first task.
  • Track itLog who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet or job safety plan, not in someone's head.

Printable

Crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the site safety lead.

  • Cool water at the yard and at every work areaPlenty of cool potable water at the yard and reachable from each work area, including upper floors and the far side of the site. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
  • Shade you can actually useOpen sites have little shade, so stage a canopy or shaded structure near the work, large enough for the crew at once. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
  • Early start, heavy work firstWhere clients and noise rules allow, schedule concrete, heavy lifting and hauling for cooler hours and leave lighter tasks for the afternoon peak. Rotate people between heavy and lighter tasks.
  • Buddy checksPair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. A worker who stops sweating or acts confused needs emergency help.
  • Clothing, PPE and sunWear the required protective gear, and use sun protection and light, breathable clothing where allowed. Hard hats, vests, gloves and long sleeves add heat load, so build in more cool-down time.
  • Emergency planWrite the job address on the crew sheet, name who calls EMS, and plan how EMS reaches a worker on a large site or an upper floor. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
  • Training in the crew's languageCover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

Tennessee context

Local / regulatory note (high level)

Employers have a general duty under federal OSHA law to protect workers from recognized hazards, including heat. Check the current federal and Tennessee requirements, and any client, property manager, or contract rules that apply to your crew, rather than relying on this page.

Construction crews often work under general contractors, owners and multiple subcontractors. Clarify in writing who provides water, shade, and training, and keep a training log.

This is general awareness only, not legal advice and not a compliance guarantee. Verify current federal and Tennessee requirements with counsel or your safety lead.

FAQ

Quick answers for Memphis construction crews

Why use WBGT instead of air temperature for construction work?

WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and air movement, not just air temperature. In hot, humid Memphis summers, that gives a better picture of crew heat load, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a stronger trigger than the forecast high.

When should we add breaks on a hot construction work day?

Build water and shade breaks into the schedule before the heat peaks, and add more as conditions move into higher bands. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance for specifics.

How much water should the crew drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, more in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your employer, client, property, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
