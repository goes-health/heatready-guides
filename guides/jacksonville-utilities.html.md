## Jacksonville summer risk: humid air, hot trucks and little shade at the pole or trench

Jacksonville summers are hot and humid, with warm nights and sea-breeze patterns that do not always cool
inland job sites. Humidity slows sweat evaporation, so the body sheds heat less effectively, and warm
nights give crews less chance to recover between shifts. Air temperature alone is a weak guide, which is
why a humidity-aware metric such as WBGT is useful.

Utility work adds its own load: climbing and bucket work in direct sun, trench and pavement heat,
heavy gear and flame-resistant or insulating clothing that traps heat, long shifts, and call-outs after
storms when crews work extended hours. Conditions at the pole or trench are often hotter than the
street-level forecast. Plan before the shift, move the heaviest work to cooler hours, and escalate using
GOES digital WBGT as sun and humidity rise.

- Humid: Slows sweat evaporation, so heat feels worse
- Exposed work: Sun on pavement, trench and bucket work; little shade at the pole
- Truck & yard: Water and a cool-down spot staged at every job

## Acclimatization ramp for new and returning crew

Heat-illness risk is highest in the first days on the job or after time away. Build the ramp into the crew schedule.

- **Days 1–2: about 20% of normal hot-work load** — Common guidance for new workers is to start at roughly 20% of the usual time in the heat and add no more than about 20% a day. Confirm against your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.
- **Returning workers after leave** — Often a faster ramp, such as about 50% on day one, 60% on day two, 80% on day three, and full load on day four. Treat anyone back from a week or more away as partly un-acclimatized.
- **Contractors and mutual-aid crews** — Seasonal hires, mutual-aid crews arriving from cooler regions and new contractors are the most at risk, especially during storm restoration. Brief them in a language they understand on day one, before the first task.
- **Track it** — Log who is in a ramp and who their buddy is. Put it on the crew sheet or job safety plan, not in someone's head.

## Crew checklist

Check these before the shift and again when the heat climbs. Print this section for the site safety lead.

- **Cool water at the truck and near each work area** — Plenty of cool potable water on every truck and reachable from the pole, trench or manhole, not only at the yard. Aim for about 1 quart (32 oz) per hour in heavy heat, in small sips every 15–20 minutes, not just when thirsty.
- **Shade you can actually use** — Poles and trenches have little shade, so stage a canopy or vehicle shade near the work, large enough for the crew at once. Breaks only count if workers cool down there.
- **Early start, heavy work first** — Where the job allows, schedule digging, climbing, lifting and material carrying for cooler hours and leave lighter tasks for the afternoon peak. Rotate people between tasks.
- **Buddy checks** — Pair workers and check for confusion, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, or stopped sweating. A worker who stops sweating or acts confused needs emergency help.
- **Clothing, PPE and sun** — Wear the required protective clothing, and use sun protection and breathable base layers where allowed. Hard hats, harnesses, gloves and flame-resistant or insulating gear raise heat load, so build in more cool-down time.
- **Emergency plan** — Write the job address on the crew sheet, name who calls EMS, and plan how EMS reaches a worker who is in a bucket, on a pole or in a trench, and how to lower or reach them quickly. Know cooling first aid before help arrives.
- **Training in the crew's language** — Cover symptoms, water, breaks, and how to report a problem without retaliation, in the languages the crew understands, including English and Spanish.

## Quick answers for Jacksonville utility crews

### Why use WBGT instead of air temperature for utility work?

WBGT accounts for humidity, sun, and air movement, not just air temperature. In hot, humid Jacksonville summers, that gives a better picture of crew heat load, which is why GOES digital WBGT is a stronger trigger than the forecast high.

### When should we add breaks on a hot utility work day?

Build water and shade breaks into the schedule before the heat peaks, and add more as conditions move into higher bands. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance for specifics.

### How much water should the crew drink?

A common guide is small sips every 15–20 minutes, more in heavy heat. Follow your written program and current OSHA / NIOSH guidance.

### Does this page make us compliant?

No. It is an educational checklist. Your obligations depend on your employer, client, property, and training. Use counsel and your safety lead for formal plans.
